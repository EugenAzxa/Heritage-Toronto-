Meet Albert Jackson

One letter carrier. One decision. One city that answered.

In the spring of 1882, a young man in Toronto was handed a mailbag, then had it taken away because of the colour of his skin. What happened next was not quiet. Neighbours organized. Newspapers argued. A prime minister weighed in. And within weeks, Albert Jackson walked out onto his route as the first Black letter carrier in the city. This is his story, told in his own voice.

The Story

A life carried north

Nine chapters, from a Delaware slave cabin to a Toronto laneway that carries his name. Scroll to follow the route.

  1. Born in bondage

    Chapter I

    Born into slavery in Delaware

    Albert Jackson was born on the second of November, 1857, in Milford, Delaware, the youngest child of Ann Maria Jackson. He was born into slavery. Freedom was not a birthright he was given. It was one his mother would risk everything to seize.

  2. The road north

    Chapter II

    Ann Maria and the Underground Railroad

    After two of her sons were seized and sold, and her husband died of grief, Ann Maria Jackson made her choice. With help from the Underground Railroad, she fled the United States with seven children. In 1858 they reached Toronto. Albert, still a toddler, arrived in a country where he could be free.

  3. Chapter III

    A Toronto childhood in St. John's Ward

    The family settled in St. John's Ward, the downtown neighbourhood that welcomed so many who had come north before the American Civil War. There Albert grew up and was educated, an ordinary Toronto boy in an extraordinary community of people who had walked out of slavery into a new life.

  4. The appointment

    Chapter IV

    Appointed a letter carrier

    On May 12, 1882, Albert Jackson was appointed a letter carrier for the Toronto Post Office. It was a respected civil service position, and one almost never given to a man of colour in nineteenth century Canada. The mailbag was his. For a moment.

  5. Chapter V

    The refusal

    His white colleagues refused to train him. Rather than teach a Black man his route, the other carriers turned their backs, and Jackson's supervisor pushed him into an indoor job as a hall porter instead. The appointment he had earned was quietly being taken away.

  6. The city speaks

    Chapter VI

    Toronto's Black community stood up

    The community would not let it stand. They organized a public meeting, formed a committee to advocate for him, and took the fight into the newspapers. A heated debate over one man's job ran across the city's press. Toronto was being asked what kind of city it wanted to be.

  7. Chapter VII

    Reinstated to his route

    With an election near and Black voters watching, Prime Minister Sir John A. Macdonald lent his support. The pressure worked. On June 2, 1882, Albert Jackson began his training. He would walk his route after all, the first Black letter carrier in Toronto.

  8. The long route

    Chapter VIII

    Thirty-six years on the route

    In 1883 Albert married Henrietta Elizabeth Jones, and together they raised four sons. Day after day, year after year, he carried the mail through Harbord Village and the Annex. In 1914 he bought a house at 213 Brunswick Avenue, a few streets from his route; his family would hold it until 1970. He served the Toronto Post Office for thirty-six years, until his death on the fourteenth of January, 1918. He is buried in the Toronto Necropolis.

  9. Not forgotten

    Chapter IX

    A name the city still carries

    More than a century on, Toronto remembers. A Heritage Toronto plaque marks the ground where he collected his mail. A downtown laneway now bears his name. And in 2019 his face travelled the country on a Canada Post stamp. The man they tried to keep off the streets is written into them.

Legacy

How Toronto remembers him

The man they tried to keep off the streets is now written into the city, in bronze, on a street sign, on a national stamp, and over the door of the plant where Toronto's mail is sorted.

2013

Albert Jackson Lane

A laneway off Harbord Street, in the Harbord Village neighbourhood he walked for decades, was officially named Albert Jackson Lane, turning his daily route to work into a permanent place on the map.

2017

A Heritage Toronto plaque

Heritage Toronto placed a plaque telling his story on the ground where the old Toronto General Post Office once stood, the very site where Jackson collected the mail he carried across the city.

2019

A national stamp

For Black History Month, Canada Post issued a commemorative stamp portraying Albert Jackson in uniform on a Toronto street, sending his story into homes across the country.

2024

A National Historic Person

The Government of Canada designated Albert Calvin Jackson a National Historic Person, recognizing one of the first mail carriers of African descent in the country's postal service.

2023

A plant that carries his name

Canada Post named its new Scarborough mail plant the Albert Jackson Processing Centre, announced in 2022 and opened the following spring. The mail he was once refused the right to carry now moves through a building named after him.

About this project

One story, told properly

This is a working demonstration, built by Saylavy around a single Heritage Toronto subject. Albert Jackson's is the story we chose. The point is that any story in the city's archive could be told this way.

A person you can ask

Visitors put questions to Albert in his own voice and hear the answers aloud. Every reply is grounded in a written record kept in one auditable file, and he is instructed to say plainly when the record does not answer. Nothing is invented.

A story placed in the world

The Atlas of Lives puts sixty-eight figures on a globe at their birthplace, Albert and his mother among them. The same globe would hold a hundred Toronto lives just as easily.

Built to archival standards

Sources are named, images are credited, and where the record is thin the site says so. It meets WCAG AA contrast throughout, works by keyboard, and respects reduced-motion settings.

Made as a tribute and as a proposal. If Heritage Toronto would like to see another subject treated this way, that is exactly the conversation we are hoping to have.
